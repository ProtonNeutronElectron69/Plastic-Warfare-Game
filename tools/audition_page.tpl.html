Before you commit

Where these came from, and why the licence is stronger this time

Every music host I would normally use — Musopen, OpenGameArt, Kenney, the Internet Archive — is blocked by this environment's network policy. GitHub is not, so these came from SoundSafari/CC0-1.0-Music, a public mirror aggregating several free-music libraries.

But the provenance here does not rest on that mirror. Twelve of the seventeen are the US Army Old Guard Fife and Drum Corps — a serving unit of the US Army, so its recordings are works of the federal government and public domain in the US under 17 USC 105, whatever any mirror claims. Three more (Edison Military Band, Arthur Pryor's Band, Prince's Orchestra) are acoustic-era sides from roughly 1900–1920, long out of copyright.

Still confirm before shipping. That argument is about where the recordings came from, not about the mirror — check the one track you actually choose at its original source.

Marches loop better than the last set did

Every candidate runs 36 seconds to 3 minutes, and still needs 30–45 seconds that joins back to its own start cut out of it. But a march is built from repeated strains of fixed length, which is exactly the structure a loop wants — far easier to cut cleanly than the through-composed cues in the first round.

One ensemble, or four

Twelve of these are the same corps, so choosing all four slots from the Old Guard would give the soundtrack one voice — the same fifes, the same drum line, the same room — which is usually worth more than picking the single best track per slot. The drum features in the combat slot are literally the same players as the menu march with the fifes taken away, and that relationship will read.

MP3 cannot loop seamlessly

This is the one technical decision that should be made before any files are cut. Every MP3 encoder pads the start and end of a file with a few milliseconds of silence, so a looped MP3 audibly ticks. Your existing 35 sounds are one-shots, where this is invisible.

Two ways out: ship the music as OGG Vorbis instead (no padding, and every browser the game already supports plays it), or keep MP3 and set explicit loop points on the Web Audio source node so the padding is skipped. The first is simpler and is what I would recommend.

What the plumbing already gives you

armsBus in source/js/03-audio.js:65 is the pattern a music bus would copy — its own gain node hanging off masterGain, so music gets a volume of its own without touching the weapons mix. And COMBAT_DUCK_T (line 1003, applied at line 1050) already pulls the mining ambience down during a firefight; pointing it at the music bus too means the score drops back under gunfire for free.

The three antique recordings are a taste decision

Uncle Sammy March, Children Toy March and General Mixup March are flagged in amber above because they are genuine acoustic-era transfers — measured at 20 dB less content above 10 kHz than the modern recordings here, which you hear as narrow, hissy 78rpm sound.

That is not a defect to fix. A wind-up-gramophone soundtrack under a game about toy soldiers on a kitchen floor is a real aesthetic, and it would sit beside the recorded gunfire in a way the clean recordings would not. But it is a decision to make deliberately, which is why they are marked rather than mixed in.
